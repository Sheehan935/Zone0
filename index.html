What Is Zone 0?

Zone 0 is the first 5 feet around your foundation. Wind-blown embers ignite houses by landing in dry fuel against exterior walls. Keeping this zone clear of combustible materials is the single most effective way to protect your home.

Does it apply to me?

Check if your home is in an official hazard zone using the CAL FIRE Fire Hazard Severity Zone Viewer.

Zone 0 · 0–5 ft Ember-resistant zone

Hardscape only. Nothing combustible against the house.

Zone 1 · 5–30 ft Lean. Green. Clean.

Low, well-spaced, well-watered plants with no ladder fuels.

Zone 2 · 30–100 ft Reduced fuel

Spaced shrubs, limbed-up trees, mowed grasses.

Ember-Defense Kits

How Embers Turn Small Vulnerabilities Into Ignitions

A home doesn’t have to be touched by the main flame front to ignite. Wind can carry burning embers ahead of a wildfire, and they can enter an opening or land in dry material beside the house. Learn where they get in, then see the materials people commonly use to close those gaps.

The Practical Idea

Reduce the places where embers can enter, collect, or find something easy to ignite. Home hardening lowers risk; it does not eliminate it.

Vents Are Necessary Openings — And Possible Entry Paths

Attic, soffit, gable, and foundation vents release heat and moisture. The same openings can admit wind-driven embers. Torn screens, open edges, and coarse mesh increase the size of the path. Screening or a purpose-built vent can reduce ember intrusion, but any change must preserve the ventilation the building needs.

Why Guidance Points to 1/8-Inch Metal Mesh

Wildfire-preparedness guidance commonly specifies noncombustible, corrosion-resistant metal mesh with openings from 1/16 inch to 1/8 inch. A 1/8-inch screen is finer than common 1/4-inch utility screening, so it can intercept smaller embers while serving as a retrofit screen. It does not stop every ember — embers vary in size, mesh can clog or corrode, and screening does not stop radiant heat or direct flame. Check fit, condition, and required net free ventilation before installation.

The First Five Feet Interrupt the Fuel Path

The area from the structure out to five feet is often called the immediate or noncombustible zone. The goal isn’t simply to spread gravel — it’s to keep embers from finding receptive fuel against walls, under windows, at deck edges, or beside vents. Noncombustible ground cover can help when dry vegetation, wood mulch, leaves, stored items, combustible fencing, and other burnable material are also removed or separated. Maintenance matters, since leaves and needles can collect on top of rock.

A Simple Property Self-Assessment

  • Start at the roofline: look for leaves and needles in gutters, roof valleys, and wall-to-roof intersections. Note damaged covers, open tile ends, and visible gaps.
  • Map every vent: photograph attic, soffit, gable, crawl-space, and foundation vents. Record dimensions, screen condition, opening size if known, and nearby combustible material.
  • Walk the exterior envelope: look at eaves, siding transitions, utility penetrations, windows, doors, and the garage-door edge for gaps or accumulated debris.
  • Measure five feet out: mark a five-foot band around the house and attached decks. Note wood mulch, dry plants, doormats, furniture, stored items, fencing, leaves, and anything else that can burn.
  • Look beneath and between: check under decks and stairs, in corners, beside planters, and where fencing or other structures connect to the house.

Photos can reveal visible priorities. They can’t confirm hidden construction, code compliance, airflow, or whether a roof or vent assembly is safe to alter. A free Photo Review can help with the visible part.

Questions to Understand Before Choosing Materials

How can an ember ignite a home?
An ember can enter an opening and ignite material inside, or land in dry leaves, wood mulch, a doormat, furniture, or another receptive fuel beside the structure. That small fire can then spread. Direct flame and radiant heat are also wildfire exposures, so ember work is one part of a broader strategy.
Why are vents a concern?
Vents are intentional openings that manage heat and moisture in attics and underfloor spaces. Wind-driven embers can use those same pathways. Screening or a purpose-built vent can reduce intrusion, but the assembly still needs adequate airflow and correct installation.
Why 1/8-inch mesh?
Wildfire guidance commonly calls for noncombustible, corrosion-resistant metal mesh with openings from 1/16 inch to 1/8 inch. A 1/8-inch screen reduces opening size compared with common 1/4-inch utility mesh. It does not stop every ember, and finer screening can affect airflow or clog, so verify ventilation requirements and maintain the screen.
What does the five-foot zone actually do?
It reduces the chance that an ember will ignite something immediately beside the structure and create flame or heat against the wall, window, vent, deck, or eave. Gravel, rock, decomposed granite, pavers, and concrete can be useful ground surfaces, but only as part of a zone kept free of dry debris and other combustible material.
Are these kits required by law?
No. These are optional examples of materials used for home-hardening and defensible-space work. Local requirements vary. Check with the appropriate local authority before work that may require permits or must meet specific rules.
Which materials fit my property?
Start with observations and measurements. Vent type, dimensions, required airflow, gutter size and length, drainage, ground-cover depth, access, and installation conditions all affect the answer. A photo review can organize visible priorities but cannot verify hidden construction.
Can I install the materials myself?
Cleaning and some small screening or edging tasks may suit an experienced homeowner. Roofline, vent, flashing, foundation, ladder, and drainage work may need a qualified contractor. Do not alter ventilation or a building assembly without understanding the consequences.
Do these materials eliminate wildfire risk?
No. They are used to reduce specific vulnerabilities. Mesh does not stop all embers, heat, or flame. Gutter guards still need cleaning. Noncombustible ground cover can collect burnable debris. Even products marketed as ember-resistant need correct fit and installation.

Landscaping, Hardscaping & Design

Planting With Purpose

Fire safety and a beautiful property aren't in conflict. Here's how planting, materials, and design work together.

Design Guidance, Not a Plant List

A fire-adapted landscape should do more than reduce fuel. It should create structure, color, texture, and continuity across the property. These reliable plant choices show how placement, spacing, maintenance, and design can work together to create a landscape that is both beautiful and better adapted to wildfire.

Structural Anchors

Give the landscape shape and backbone. Manzanita 'Dr. Hurd' sheds embers off its smooth bark and needs excellent drainage, avoiding summer irrigation once established. Toyon plays the same role at a larger scale — broad, moist leaves that tend to resist ignition better than many chaparral shrubs, spaced at least 10 ft apart in individual stands.

Pollinator Accents

Bring color and pollinator activity to the landscape. California Buckwheat is fine-textured and tightly packed, a standard choice in defensible-space plantings. California Lilac 'Concha' adds spring blue blooms with dense, moisture-rich foliage when properly spaced.

Groundcovers & Spillers

Soften edges, borders, and containers while keeping planting intentional around the home. Canyon Live-Forever is a compact, moisture-rich succulent well suited to containers. Common Yarrow works as a low-water lawn substitute — mowing it flat helps keep fuel volume low and foliage moisture relatively high.

Native Integration

Tie designed planting into the surrounding landscape. California Sagebrush is fine and aromatic, kept well-spaced from neighboring shrubs. Coast Live Oak, pruned to clear ladder fuel beneath it, brings thick, moist bark.

FAQ & Official Resources

Regulatory status last verified September 22, 2026

Is Zone 0 final yet? When do these rules take effect?
Not yet. On August 19, 2026, the California Board of Forestry and Fire Protection voted unanimously to approve final Zone 0 regulations — the first statewide ember-resistant-zone rule in the country. The package was submitted to the state Office of Administrative Law on August 28, 2026 as emergency rulemaking, but it was withdrawn from OAL review on September 8, 2026 before taking effect. There is currently no statewide effective date and no Zone 0 compliance clock running. Existing fire-safety, brush-clearance, and vegetation-management requirements still apply in the meantime. We'll update this page as soon as the rule is resubmitted or takes effect.
What's the compliance timeline for existing homes?
There's no active deadline right now — as covered in the previous question, the final rule was withdrawn from state review before taking effect, so no statewide compliance clock is running yet. Once it does take effect, reporting on the rule describes two phases from that date: within roughly 3 years, clear the first 5 ft of firewood, dead leaves and branches, bark mulch, and wood chips, keep gutters and roofs clear, and finish any overdue tree work; within roughly 5 years, create vegetation-free buffers under eaves, windows, and vents, replace combustible fence and gate sections where they attach to the house, and address sheds and other attached combustible structures. Enforcement is expected to be education-first in the early years — the goal is measurable progress, not a single hard deadline. Existing fire-safety and vegetation-management requirements still apply regardless. Our First-5-Feet To-Do List walks through these same items in order, and a free Photo Review can show you where your property stands today, regardless of the regulatory timeline.
What is Assembly Bill 3074 (AB 3074)?
AB 3074 requires the Board of Forestry and Fire Protection to establish regulations for an ember-resistant zone (Zone 0) within 0 to 5 feet of structures in Very High Fire Hazard Severity Zones (VHFHSZ).
Are bark and wood mulch allowed in Zone 0?
No. Organic mulches such as wood chips, bark, and pine needles ignite easily when struck by windborne embers. They must be replaced with non-combustible materials like gravel, decomposed granite (DG), pavers, or bare earth.

Do I have to remove all plants from the 5-foot perimeter?
Not necessarily. While minimizing plants directly adjacent to walls is recommended, low-growing, high-moisture specimens planted selectively with non-combustible groundcover separation are allowed depending on local fire authority enforcement.
