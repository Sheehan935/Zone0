Core Principles

The Science of Ember Dynamics & Ignition Vulnerability

During a wildfire, it is rarely a giant wall of flame that burns down a house. It is the invisible blizzard of burning embers — firebrands — blowing miles ahead of the fire front. Nationwide research attributes the large majority of home losses in wildland-urban interface fires to embers, not direct flame contact.

What AB 3074 Actually Requires

Assembly Bill 3074 (2020) directs California's Board of Forestry and Fire Protection to adopt building code regulations establishing an "ember-resistant zone" — Zone 0 — within 0 to 5 feet of structures in Very High Fire Hazard Severity Zones (VHFHSZ) and Wildland-Urban Interface areas. The statute sets the mandate; the Board defines the technical standard, which is why cities like Berkeley and fire districts like Moraga-Orinda have moved ahead with their own local Zone 0 ordinances in the meantime.

0–5 ft Regulated ring

The 0–5 ft ring exists because it is the zone where three failure modes converge at once: it's close enough to the house for an ember to reach an ignition point without traveling far, it's where combustible landscaping (mulch, wood fencing, stored firewood) is most commonly placed right up against a wall, and it's where the structure's own architecture — corners, vents, decks — creates places for wind-blown embers to physically collect and sit until they ignite something.

Ember Trap Hydraulics

Embers don't fall in a straight line — once airborne, a lofted firebrand behaves less like a solid object and more like a particle suspended in a moving fluid, governed by the same boundary-layer aerodynamics that pile snow into drifts. As wind moves across a building, it separates and recirculates at obstructions: inside walls, at re-entrant corners, in the low-pressure eddy behind a chimney or dormer, and along the toe of a wall where horizontal wind meets a vertical surface. Airflow slows dramatically in these recirculation zones, and embers — which are light relative to their surface area — lose momentum and settle out of the airstream exactly there. This is why the highest ember accumulation on a real structure is almost never on an open, flat, wind-swept surface; it's concentrated in inside corners, stairwells, deck-to-wall junctions, and gutter valleys — the same low-flow pockets where blowing snow or dry leaves collect.

Because these "ember traps" are a predictable consequence of the building's geometry, they are also predictable places to defend: any organic fuel (mulch, leaf litter, stacked wood) sitting inside one of these pockets is sitting exactly where an ember is statistically most likely to land and dwell long enough to ignite it.

Wind-Borne Firebrand Dynamics

A firebrand is a burning or smoldering piece of vegetation or structural debris lofted by a fire's convective plume. Depending on size, density, and plume strength, firebrands have been documented traveling well over a mile ahead of an active fire front in wind-driven conditions — a phenomenon fire scientists call "spotting." Smaller, lighter embers travel farther but carry less stored heat and cool faster in flight; larger embers carry more heat but fall out of the airstream sooner. The combined effect during a wind event is a firebrand "shower": a sustained rain of embers of mixed size arriving well before, and often independent of, the main fire, which is why a home can ignite from embers alone even if the flame front never reaches the property line.

This is also why Zone 0 is treated as a distinct hazard category from the broader defensible-space zones farther out: at 30–100 ft (Zone 2), the goal is reducing fuel that could carry flame toward the house; at 0–5 ft, flame contact from adjacent vegetation is only part of the risk — the zone must also survive an ember shower arriving independently, by air, regardless of what is or isn't burning nearby.

Siding & Envelope Ignition Points

Once an ember lands and holds heat against a building surface, ignition depends on what that surface is and where the embers can penetrate the building envelope:

  • Combustible siding (wood, engineered wood, some fiber-cement trims): can smolder and ignite directly from sustained ember contact, especially in corners where debris accumulates against the material.
  • Vinyl siding: typically softens and deforms at temperatures well below active ignition, warping and creating gaps that expose the water-resistive barrier and wall cavity behind it to further ember intrusion — a secondary ignition pathway rather than a primary one.
  • Vent penetrations: attic, foundation, crawlspace, and eave vents are engineered gaps in the building envelope. Standard insect screen is not ember-resistant; embers pass through easily and can ignite insulation, framing, or stored debris on the other side.
  • Deck and siding junctions: the gap where a deck ledger board meets the house, and the space beneath an elevated deck, are both wind-sheltered ember traps directly against combustible framing.
  • Window and door reveals, exterior corners, and utility penetrations: any gap, reveal, or 90° corner along the envelope is a place embers can lodge against combustible trim or accumulate as debris over time.
